Areas of care

One place for cornea, cataract and vision correction

Many eye problems involve more than one part of the eye. A person with a corneal scar may also have a cataract, and a person with keratoconus may need both a cornea treatment and a vision plan. These are the conditions I see most often.

Keratoconus

The cornea thins and bulges into a cone, which blurs vision and causes frequent changes in glasses power. Early treatment aims to stop it from progressing.

  • Corneal collagen cross-linking (CXL) to strengthen the cornea
  • Femtosecond laser assisted stromal lenticule insertion, followed by CXL
  • Deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) for advanced disease

Corneal infections

A corneal ulcer can damage vision permanently if it is not treated early. Treatment depends on the organism and how deep the infection has reached.

  • Evaluation and treatment of corneal ulcers and ocular surface disease
  • Tissue glue patch for corneal perforation
  • Therapeutic keratoplasty when medicines alone cannot clear the infection

Corneal scars

Scars left by infection, injury or earlier disease can cloud the visual axis. The right option depends on how deep the scar goes.

  • Optical penetrating keratoplasty
  • DALK and femtosecond assisted sutureless anterior lamellar keratoplasty
  • Combined planning when a cataract or high refractive error is also present

Corneal decompensation

When the inner cell layer of the cornea fails, fluid builds up and the cornea becomes swollen and hazy. It can follow cataract surgery, lens implants or inherited disease.

  • DSAEK, including femtosecond laser assisted DSAEK
  • DMEK, which replaces only the thin inner layer
  • Full thickness and tectonic keratoplasty where needed

Refractive surgery

Surgery to reduce dependence on glasses or contact lenses, after a detailed check that your cornea is suitable.

  • SMILE (refractive lenticule extraction)
  • Femtosecond LASIK
  • Phakic IOL implantation for high powers or thin corneas

Cataract surgery

Clouding of the natural lens. Surgery replaces it with an artificial lens chosen to suit your eye and daily needs.

  • Phacoemulsification, including femtosecond laser assisted cataract surgery (FLACS)
  • Premium IOL implantation
  • Paediatric cataract surgery
  • Complex cases: capsular tension rings and segments, scleral fixated IOL, pupilloplasty, iridodialysis repair

Care is planned around the whole eye. If your cornea problem also needs a cataract operation or a refractive plan, it can be worked out in the same consultation rather than across several referrals.

How the cornea is treated

The layer that is affected decides the treatment

The cornea is about half a millimetre thick and has distinct layers. Modern surgery often replaces or reshapes only the layer that is diseased, which can mean faster recovery and fewer complications than replacing the whole cornea.

Surface: epithelium and Bowman’s layer

The outer skin of the eye and its thin supporting layer

Problems
Surface infections, erosions, early scars
Treatment
Medical treatment, tissue glue for perforations, surface laser (PRK)

Stroma

The thick middle layer that gives the cornea its shape and strength

Problems
Keratoconus, deeper scars, deep infections
Treatment
Cross-linking, stromal lenticule insertion, DALK, full thickness transplant, and the reshaping done in LASIK and SMILE

Descemet’s membrane and endothelium

The innermost layer, a pump that keeps the cornea clear

Problems
Corneal decompensation, swelling, haze
Treatment
DSAEK or DMEK, which replace only this layer

For patients

When to see a cornea specialist

Consider a visit if you have

  • Vision that stays blurry or distorted even with new glasses
  • Glasses power that keeps changing, especially in a young adult
  • A white or grey spot on the eye, or a scar from an old injury or infection
  • Morning blur, haze or glare that slowly worsens, especially after cataract surgery
  • Light sensitivity, or an eye that is repeatedly red or watery
  • Interest in laser vision correction and a wish to know if your eyes are suitable

See an eye doctor the same day if your eye becomes red and painful, light bothers you, or vision drops suddenly, particularly if you wear contact lenses or had an eye injury. Remove contact lenses and avoid home remedies or leftover eye drops.

Common questions

Does keratoconus always need a transplant?

No. Most people never need one. If it is found early, cross-linking can stop it from getting worse. A transplant is kept for advanced disease or scarring.

Can anyone have laser vision correction?

No. It needs stable glasses power, a healthy and sufficiently thick cornea and no signs of keratoconus. A corneal scan decides whether laser surgery, a phakic lens or no surgery suits you.

Will cataract surgery fix my corneal problem?

Not by itself. The cornea and lens are treated separately, but when both need care, planning them together helps choose the right lens and the right order of surgery.

What should I bring to the first visit?

Your current glasses, any previous reports or scans, a list of your medicines and eye drops, and contact lenses if you use them.
